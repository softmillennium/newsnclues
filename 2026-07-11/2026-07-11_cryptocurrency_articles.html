Strategy Says It Might Sell Up to $1.25 Billion in Bitcoin. So Is MSTR a Buy, Sell, or Hold Right Now?
Source: {'href': 'https://www.fool.com', 'title': 'The Motley Fool'}    Published: 2026-07-11 09:44:15

Strategy (MSTR +0.80%) had a simple business model; it basically issued stock, preferred stock, and debt, and used the proceeds to buy Bitcoin (BTC 0.31%). That model appears to be broken following Bitcoin's deep price decline, and investors should be worried about the implications. To be fair, Strategy is making the right move by shifting from a buy-and-hold model to a more dynamic one, noting that it may sell up to $1.25 billion in Bitcoin. It also wants to build a cash reserve roughly equal to 12 months of preferred stock and interest payments. A pivot was necessary and shows the company is willing to evolve along with the evolving cryptocurrency market. From a big-picture view, however, investors buying Strategy likely see it as a way to buy Bitcoin without actually buying Bitcoin. But the massive decline in the price of Bitcoin, which has lost 40% of its value over the past year and is now down around 50% from its high in 2025, is a troubling sign. Cryptocurrency lovers may have moved on to other options, like prediction markets. Notably, Robinhood (HOOD 2.73%) saw its crypto revenue fall nearly 50% in the first quarter of 2026, while revenue from prediction markets rose 320%. However, you could also argue that this business shift is a sign that the current drawdown is too much for even Strategy to absorb as more and more traders move on to the next hot fad. To be fair, Bitcoin has always been volatile, with the deepest drawdown exceeding 80%. If you are a long-term believer in Bitcoin, you might want to hold on to your Strategy shares or, perhaps, buy more. An investment in Strategy is a way to outsource your Bitcoin "investments" without doing that work yourself, but you have to recognize the material risks you are taking on. Simply buying and holding Bitcoin clearly isn't a workable long-term strategy, since a company built around that business model was forced to change. More conservative investors, however, should probably view Strategy's model shift as an indication that Bitcoin may have been an investment fad. For most investors, the answer will probably be that it isn't, and that means Strategy won't be worth owning, either. Reuben Gregg Brewer has no position in any of the stocks mentioned. US Housing Bill Barring Fed CBDC Becomes Law Without Trump Signature
Source: {'href': 'https://en.bloomingbit.io', 'title': 'bloomingbit'}    Published: 2026-07-11 05:45:40

A bipartisan US housing bill, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, has become law without President Donald Trump's signature. The legislation includes a provision that bars the Federal Reserve from issuing a central bank digital currency, or CBDC. Trump wrote on Truth Social that he would not sign the housing bill even though Congress had approved it. Despite Trump's opposition, the bill won overwhelming support in Congress. Its main goal is to lower housing supply costs by promoting home construction and expanding financing options. Senior officials, however, have remained skeptical and have maintained that they would not issue a CBDC without explicit congressional approval. Blocking a CBDC has long been one of Republican lawmakers' top priorities. Last year, a stablecoin bill known as the GENIUS Act also failed after Republicans opposed it because it did not include a blanket CBDC ban. With the housing bill now in effect, the Fed's inability to issue a CBDC has been formally written into law. Bitcoin halving cycle history challenges $300,000–$500,000 moonshot forecasts
Source: {'href': 'https://www.coindesk.com', 'title': 'CoinDesk'}    Published: 2026-07-11 02:31:05

Crypto analysts are already looking at how high bitcoin BTC$64,209.91 could rally in the next cycle, expected to begin later this year, with ambitious targets ranging from $300,000 to $500,000. But one important data point runs counter to those forecasts, suggesting that gains may be more measured than ever. Unlike gold or stocks, bitcoin tends to move in clear four-year cycles centered on the mining reward halving, an event that halves the amount of new bitcoin produced per block every 4 years. In the past, prices have tended to bottom out and begin a new bull run roughly 18 months before the halving. On the back of this, analysts and market experts have been calling for a massive bull run in the next few years. Veteran trader Peter Brandt anticipates a peak between $300,000 and $500,000. Bernstein analysts Gautam Chhugani and Mahika Sapra expect prices to hit $500,000 by 2029, citing booming demand for spot exchange-traded funds (ETFs). However, while the four-year cycles have consistently produced new all-time highs, each successive one has seen markedly lower multiples, compressed gains, and slower overall expansion. The track record of cycle highs proves it: What this means is that bull runs are getting steadier, with more measured gains rather than moonshots. Bulls might argue that a potential full-blown Fed stimulus and outright purchases of BTC as a reserve asset by the U.S. Treasury may do the trick. However, even massive fiscal and monetary stimulus after the 2020 COVID crash – not just in the U.S. but worldwide – could only lift BTC to nearly $70,000 in that cycle, 3.5x from 2017, marking a slowdown from the previous cycle. All this suggests that bitcoin is maturing and growing, not breaking. The days of peak-to-peak moonshots may be gone for good. Jacksonville's Business Infrastructure Boom Is Starting To Overlap With Blockchain Data
Source: {'href': 'https://www.jacksonville.com', 'title': 'The Florida Times-Union'}    Published: 2026-07-11 01:10:03

Increasingly, some businesses are also watching the broader top cryptocurrency market to monitor where digital payment infrastructure and blockchain-based coordination tools may be gaining traction. That does not mean local executives are suddenly turning into crypto traders. The conversation has become more focused on infrastructure maturity, payment systems, and operational utility than market hype. One reason digital asset data keeps showing up in business conversations is that the numbers can reveal behavior long before a technology becomes mainstream. Volume, liquidity, market capitalization, transaction activity, and network participation can help businesses gauge whether a blockchain ecosystem is attracting enough support to remain operationally relevant. That does not automatically make a digital asset trustworthy or practical. For a logistics-heavy region like Northeast Florida, that distinction matters. A warehouse operator near the port may eventually encounter software vendors offering blockchain-linked shipment records or tokenized invoicing systems. A freight forwarder could see payment platforms experimenting with faster supplier settlement workflows tied to stablecoin infrastructure. In those situations, market participation becomes a rough indicator of whether supporting infrastructure is actually growing. The surrounding network is what businesses are really evaluating. The World Economic Forum's 2025 report on asset tokenization discusses digital ownership models tied to issuance, securities financing, and asset management. Instead of treating blockchain systems purely as speculative assets, the report examines how tokenized infrastructure may support programmable records, shared transaction systems, and more flexible coordination between financial participants. That shift matters because large institutions rarely move quickly unless they see operational potential somewhere underneath the hype. The result is that blockchain tools are increasingly embedded within existing business systems rather than outside them. Vendors build verification layers tied to digital identity or transaction records. Most businesses may not even notice the transition happening at first. A port-adjacent logistics company, for instance, could eventually work with systems designed to verify delivery records between suppliers, warehouses, trucking firms, and insurers. It would be to reduce friction when multiple organizations need synchronized information. A hotel group or tourism-facing business might explore prepaid digital credits or loyalty-linked rewards if vendors provide reliable dollar settlement and customer support. None of those systems would matter simply because blockchain is involved. For local businesses, those concerns become practical very quickly. Can a payment processor reliably convert transactions into dollars? What happens if a vendor loses technical support or changes compliance standards? Most businesses already manage customer databases, payroll systems, payment processors, booking platforms, and vendor software. Introducing another infrastructure layer only works if the security standards are genuinely strong. A surprising number of blockchain projects still operate with thin liquidity, weak governance, unclear disclosures, or limited real-world adoption. Others may disappear before businesses ever find a practical use for them. Jacksonville's economy is deeply tied to logistics, healthcare, tourism, transportation, and service coordination, so businesses tend to evaluate technology through a practical lens. Does it make payments, records, or customer interactions easier to manage? That mindset may ultimately matter more than hype cycles or market swings. As blockchain infrastructure slowly filters into payment systems, vendor software, and operational tools, many Northeast Florida businesses are likely to judge it the same way they judge every other technology decision: by whether it solves a real-world business problem.
