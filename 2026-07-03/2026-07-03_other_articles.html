SETI releases the most important document about extraterrestrial intelligence you will probably never read
Source: {'href': 'https://www.earth.com', 'title': 'Earth.com'}    Published: 2026-07-03 01:51:46

There has long been a quiet plan for how scientists would handle the biggest discovery in human history. It was written for a world that no longer exists – so researchers just tore it up and started over. You have no way to know. That exact scenario is what keeps SETI scientists up at night. Which is why, on June 5, the International Academy of Astronautics (IAA) quietly released the most consequential document you'll probably never read: a rewritten set of protocols telling scientists what to do – and just as importantly, what not to do – if they ever detect evidence of intelligent life beyond Earth. It's the first major overhaul of these rules since 2010, back when nobody had heard of deepfakes and Twitter was still a place you posted your lunch. His team spent roughly three years, from 2022 to 2025, rewriting the so-called post-detection protocols before the IAA formally ratified them this month. “The information environment we operate in today is vastly more complex than it was in 2010,” said Garrett. “In an era of deepfakes, automated misinformation, and instant global connectivity, a single unverified claim could trigger confusion or panic. These new protocols ensure that scientists maintain the highest standards of evidence before making announcements to the world.” Think about how fast a rumor moves now compared to fifteen years ago. A candidate signal leaks, someone posts it, and within an hour the story has mutated beyond recognition. The old protocols simply had no answer for that. At the core of the new declaration sits an old idea, one Carl Sagan loved to repeat: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Under the updated rules, a scientist who spots a promising signal shouldn't breathe a public word about a discovery until it has been independently verified – ideally by multiple facilities, run by different organizations, using different instruments and methods. The declaration is blunt that this could take a long time, and that follow-up work might never produce a definitive answer either way. Only when we have reached a consensus that a signal is credible do we bring it to the world.” There's a pressure valve built in, though. If rumors start flying about an unconfirmed candidate, teams are encouraged to speak up and explain what's actually being analyzed, rather than let the vacuum fill with nonsense. Here's something that has genuinely changed since 2010: what scientists are even looking for. Researchers now hunt across the entire electromagnetic spectrum for what they call technosignatures – observable evidence of alien technology. That includes optical laser flashes, strange anomalies in astronomical data, even excess infrared heat that could betray enormous energy-hungry structures built around distant stars. The new declaration explicitly covers all of it, including physical artifacts. One thing it pointedly does not cover, however, is UAP – the unidentified anomalous phenomena reported in Earth's atmosphere that have dominated headlines and congressional hearings in recent years. The document draws that line in its very first footnote. Anyone attached to a potential detection could face harassment, doxxing, or a media siege, and the declaration says institutions must take steps to shield their researchers. The report should be peer-reviewed, published with all the underlying data, and archived in at least two repositories in different parts of the world. The declaration holds firm on a principle that has anchored SETI thinking for decades: no reply should be sent to an extraterrestrial intelligence until humanity, as a whole, decides one should be. “The release of these updated rules and protocols marks an important step in acknowledging both the radically different media landscape that science functions within today, and the vastly expanded efforts in terms of technology and resources being deployed in the search for intelligent life beyond Earth” said Bill Diamond, President and CEO of the SETI Institute and IAA SETI Committee member. “We applaud Prof Garrett's leadership in developing these new protocols and the IAA for their ratification.” Their power comes from consensus, from the fact that the serious players in the field have agreed this is how it should be done. A permanent Post-Detection Sub-Committee, staffed with experts in law, ethics, and social science, will keep advising on what a confirmed discovery would mean for society. So the next time your feed erupts with a claim that we've found them, remember there's now a playbook. If the scientists are following it, the real announcement won't arrive as a shaky viral video at 2 a.m. It will arrive checked, double-checked, and checked again by rivals on the other side of the planet. And until that day comes, the most disciplined thing anyone in this field can do is stay quiet – and keep listening.
